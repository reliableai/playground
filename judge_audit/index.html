Judge Auditor: who is judging?

The same ten answers from an HR-policy assistant, graded by five judge prompts × three models, five times each. 750 real judgments, precomputed. The HR expert's verdict is fixed in advance; the question is how far each judge lands from it, and whether it lands in the same place twice.

Uncertainty dimension: judge bias, judge noise, and the rubric itself. Companion to Metrics and measurement, Part II.

The setup

The system under test is E4rag: an assistant that answers employee questions from a (fictional) HR handbook. Its outputs are free text, so there is no label to match. Instead each answer gets a three-row scorecard: faithfulness (pass/fail: nothing in the answer that the policy does not support), completeness (1–5) and conciseness (1–5).

Ten answers were generated and then curated so that each one exercises a specific way a judge can go wrong: a fabricated number, a correct-but-incomplete answer, a correct refusal, a verbose correct answer, a true answer to the wrong question. An HR expert graded each one before any LLM judge was run: that is the oracle.

How close is each judge to the HR expert?

      
Fifty judgments per cell (10 answers × 5 repeats). Faithfulness: share of runs whose pass/fail matches the oracle. Completeness and conciseness: mean absolute distance from the oracle score, in points on the 1–5 scale. Lower is better for the last two.

      

      
Reading the table. Move down a column and the prompt changes with the model fixed; move across a row and the model changes with the prompt fixed. Both moves change the numbers, sometimes by more than the difference between two systems you might be comparing. The judge is part of the system.

A trap in the first table. P5, the deliberately biased prompt, has the best faithfulness agreement. Seven of the ten answers are faithful, and P5 tells the judge to fail only on outright contradiction, so it says "pass" a lot and is right a lot. Raw agreement rewards a lenient judge whenever the base rate is lopsided; that is why the deck's kappa slide exists, and why the completeness and conciseness columns tell the opposite story about P5.

Noise: does the same judge give the same score twice?

      
Bias is the judge being systematically off; noise is the judge disagreeing with itself. Here, for each judge, the share of (answer, metric) pairs where the five repeats did not all agree.

      

      
Why this matters. If you score a system once with a noisy judge, the number you report has an error bar you never measured. Repeats, or a larger test set, are how you find out how wide it is. See the LLM as a Judge dials for the simulated version of this.
