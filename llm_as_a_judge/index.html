LLM as a Judge

L3 lab · an LLM can be the thing that judges your AI's answers — classifying them or scoring them. In L1 the judge was perfect; a real judge has two defects, bias and noise.

Uncertainty dimension: judge noise & bias.

An LLM as a classifier

An LLM can classify, exactly like the trained models in L1 — read a piece of text and emit one label (POSITIVE / NEGATIVE, or one of several categories). No training: a prompt with the allowed classes and a few labelled examples. This demo moved here from the L1 lab — same datasets, real precomputed LLM predictions; spin freely, no API calls.

Because we hold the true labels, we can measure the LLM exactly like L1 measured the trained model: accuracy, per-class precision/recall, the confusion matrix. And the confusion matrix shows the direction of the misses — the bias — visible only because we have ground truth.

The judge gives a number — a scorecard

      
Gen-AI answers are rarely just right or wrong. So a scoring judge rates quality on a scale, usually across several
         dimensions — a scorecard. Here an AI summarizes a real court case, and a second LLM scores that
         summary against an expert reference on three dimensions (1–5):


      

        
Accuracy · Completeness · Fluency → Overall

        

          
DimensionWhat it asks the judgeExample

          
            
Accuracyare the stated facts correct — no invented or contradicted claims?3

            
Completenessdoes it capture the parties, the claims, the outcome?4

            
Fluencyis it clear, well-formed, professional prose?2

            
Overallcomputed, not judged: 0.45·A + 0.45·C + 0.10·F, rounded to the nearest ½3.5

          
        

        
Overall is a fixed rule, not a fourth judgment.
           0.45·3 + 0.45·4 + 0.10·2 = 3.35 → 3.5. Substance (accuracy, completeness) dominates; fluency barely moves
           it — for a capable model fluency is near-constant anyway. This is the example from slide 11 of the deck.

RMSE — how bias and noise combine (on the 1–5 rating scale, where the dials live)

        

          

            
            
Both bars on the 1–5 rating scale — the gap between them is what noise adds.

          

          

            

              predicted RMSE = √(bias² +
              noise²) = –

            
measured RMSE over the sample:
              –

            
Bias and noise add in quadrature: RMSE² = mean error² + noise².
              So the RMSE bar is never shorter than the
              |mean error| bar — the extra height is the
              noise. Slide
              bias → both bars rise together; raise
              noise → only RMSE grows (noise averages out of the
              mean error). The measured RMSE can differ from the predicted √(bias²+noise²) because it's one
              finite re-roll and ratings clamp at 1–5.

          

        

      


      

        Try this: set noise to 0 and slide bias — the measured score
        slides off 3.8 and stays there however many times you re-judge. Now set bias to 0 and raise
        noise, then hit Re-judge a few times — because we average over the
        whole pool, the number stays close to 3.8 (but RMSE climbs). At high
        noise it drifts down a little, because ratings clamp at 5 and a top-heavy pool loses the upward jitter.
        Noise mostly averages out of the mean; bias does not — and RMSE sees both.

Three sources compound: sampling noise, judge noise, and bias

      
Now make it realistic. A real judge returns an integer 1–5 score, and three things move the number
         you finally report: the judge is noisy (it sometimes flips a score up or down); the judge may be
         biased (it systematically bumps a fraction of scores in one direction); and you only score a finite
         sample. The three charts below pull these apart — the scores the judge handed out,
         the per-answer error (its offset from zero is the bias, its width is the noise), and the
         bootstrap interval for the mean (where the true average really sits). Watch how bias shifts the error
         chart off zero — and how the 1–5 ceiling quietly trims it.

Bootstrapping the means

      
Same noisy, biased judge on a finite sample — but here we focus on one question: given the scores
         you observed, where could the true mean be? Resample those scores with replacement thousands of
         times, take the mean each time, and read the spread. Turn up noise or shrink the test
         set and the interval widens; slide bias and the whole interval slides off the truth — and
         the bootstrap can't see it.

Judge playbook

      

        The simulator showed the failure modes. In a real eval, use a small trusted slice to
        measure the judge, then improve the rubric and measure again. This is the practical loop:
        audit → revise → re-audit → report the remaining uncertainty.
      

      

        
        
        
        
        
      

    


    

      
Measure a judge

      
Before trusting a judge on the full test set, audit it on examples where you have a trusted reference: human labels,
         gold answers, or an expert score. Keep this slice separate from the final eval if you use it to tune the rubric.

      

        
A minimal judge audit

        

          
CheckHow to measure itWhat it tells you

          
            
Trusted sliceLabel or score 50-200 representative examples with humans or references.The yardstick. Without it, bias is invisible.

            
BiasFor scores: mean(judge - trusted). For labels: confusion matrix by true class.Whether the judge is systematically generous, harsh, or tilted toward a label.

            
NoiseRe-run the same inputs K times; record score std, label flip rate, or within-1 agreement.How much the judge wobbles when nothing else changed.

            
AgreementLabels: accuracy, F1, Cohen's kappa. Scores: MAE, correlation, within +/-1 point.Whether the judge tracks the trusted scorer closely enough for the decision.

            
SegmentsRepeat the above by class, length, language, difficulty, model source, and safety-critical cases.Where a good aggregate hides a bad judge.

            
CalibrationFit a simple correction on part of the trusted slice, then test it on a held-out part.Whether raw judge scores can be mapped onto the trusted scale.

          
        

      

      
Calibration does not make the judge objective. It tells you how the judge lines up with a trusted slice,
        and where it still fails.

    


    

      
Build a better judge

      
A good judge is a measurement instrument. Treat the rubric, prompt, examples, output schema, and model choice as
         versioned parts of that instrument.

      

        

          
Rubric design

          

            
              
Define the constructName what quality means for this task; do not mix unrelated goals in one score.

              
Anchor the scaleSay what 1, 3, and 5 mean; include short examples at each level.

              
Separate dimensionsScore correctness, completeness, safety, and fluency separately when they can disagree.

              
Say what not to rewardFor example: do not reward length, confidence, formatting, or model identity.

            
          

        

        

          
Prompt and workflow

          

            
              
Use structured outputReturn JSON: score, evidence, failure mode, confidence, and optional abstain.

              
Require evidenceAsk for the criterion or short quote that justifies the score.

              
Blind the judgeHide model identity; randomize A/B order for comparisons.

              
Version and re-auditEvery prompt/model/rubric change creates a new judge that needs a new calibration check.

            
          

        

      

    


    

      
Worked example

      
Suppose you judge support replies on a 1-5 scale. You build a trusted slice of 120 replies scored by humans, then
         run the LLM judge on the same replies.

Next move: add "do not reward length," add anchor examples for terse but correct replies, then re-audit
          on a held-out trusted slice. If needed, report both raw and calibrated scores, for example
          calibrated = 0.82 * raw + 0.37.

      

      

        
Example rubric skeleton

        
You are judging whether a support reply resolves the customer's need.

Score 1-5:
1 = wrong, unsafe, or unrelated
3 = partially helpful but incomplete
5 = fully resolves the issue, accurate, concise

Do not reward length. Reward only correctness, completeness, and usefulness.
If the answer lacks enough information, say so.

Return JSON:
{
  "score": 1-5,
  "evidence": "short criterion or quote used",
  "failure_mode": "none|wrong|incomplete|unsafe|unclear",
  "confidence": "low|medium|high"
}

The L3 takeaway

      
    
        
  1. Bias and noise are different defects. Noise is random and mostly averages out; bias is
          systematic and persists. (One catch from the compounds tab: on a bounded, top-heavy scale like 1–5, heavy noise
          hitting the ceiling can itself add a small downward bias.) Telling them apart is the first move.
    2. 
        
  2. Both widen your real uncertainty — differently. Noise widens the interval you can measure.
          Bias adds an offset your interval can't even see, so the truth may lie outside a confident-looking CI.
    3. 
        
  3. They call for different fixes. Reduce noise by averaging repeats, lowering temperature,
          and writing a crisper rubric. Reduce bias only by auditing the judge against trusted labels — calibrating
          a "judge of judges" (the thread we pick up in L6).
    4. 
        
  4. Label or number, the same two defects apply. And with no ground truth you can only see
          noise (re-run consistency) — measuring bias always needs a trusted slice to calibrate against.
    5.
