Standard deviation, standard error, and the 95% CI

You ran the eval once: N examples, S of them correct. People draw three different “error bars” from those two numbers, and they mean three different things. Enter S and N and see all three, as numbers and as bars on the same axis.

Uncertainty dimension: sample size · Companion to II.1

How to read and interact with this page
  1. Enter what you observed. S correct out of N, or click a preset. Everything on the page follows from those two numbers.
  2. Read the three bars. All three sit around the same observed accuracy. The SD bar is the spread of the individual correct/wrong outcomes; the SE bar is how much the accuracy itself would wobble on a fresh test set; the 95% CI is the interval the textbook recipe produces.
  3. Read the three cards. Each gives the number, the formula and the sentence you are allowed to say about it.
  4. Grow N. Click 4 / 5, then 80 / 100, then 8,000 / 10,000, and watch which bars shrink. The SD bar does not; that is why it should not be on an accuracy chart.

The experiment

Observed accuracy —

Three error bars from the same two numbers

Dot: observed accuracy. Bars clipped at 0% and 100% show an arrow where they run past the edge.

Standard deviation

Spread of the individual correct/wrong outcomes.

SD = √( p̂ (1 − p̂) )

—

Fixed by p̂ alone: it tells you nothing the accuracy did not already tell you, and it does not shrink as N grows. An SD bar on an accuracy chart is almost always a mistake. (An SD across repeated runs or folds is a different quantity and can be legitimate.)

Standard error

How much the observed accuracy would scatter across fresh test sets of size N.

SE = SD / √N

—

A statement about the estimate, not about individual answers. It shrinks with √N: 100× the data buys 10× the precision.

95% confidence interval

The interval the recipe produces from this one eval.

Wilson score interval

—

Not: “95% probability the true accuracy is in here”. But: “repeat the eval; 95% of the intervals built this way catch the true accuracy.” A property of the recipe, not of this interval. For a probability about the accuracy you need a belief: see Estimating Accuracy.
