Standard deviation, standard error, and the 95% CI

You ran the eval once: N examples, S of them correct. People draw three different “error bars” from those two numbers, and they mean three different things. Enter S and N and see all three, as numbers and as bars on the same axis.

Uncertainty dimension: sample size · Companion to II.1

How to read and interact with this page
  1. Enter what you observed. S correct out of N, or click a preset. Everything on the page follows from those two numbers; nothing is random until the last section.
  2. Read the three bars. All three sit around the same observed accuracy. The SD bar is the spread of the individual correct/wrong outcomes; the SE bar is how much the accuracy itself would wobble on a fresh test set; the 95% CI is the interval the textbook recipe produces.
  3. Read the three cards. Each gives the number, the formula and the sentence you are allowed to say about it.
  4. Grow N. Click 4 / 5, then 80 / 100, then 8,000 / 10,000, and watch which bars shrink. The SD bar does not; that is why it should not be on an accuracy chart.
  5. Test the recipe. The last section pretends the truth is your observed accuracy, repeats the eval many times, and counts how often the 95% CI catches it. Start with 20 repeats, then 10,000. Switch to Wald at N = 5 to see the textbook interval fail.

Observed accuracy —

Three error bars from the same two numbers

Dot: observed accuracy. Bars clipped at 0% and 100% show an arrow where they run past the edge.

Standard deviation

Spread of the individual correct/wrong outcomes.

SD = √( p̂ (1 − p̂) )

—

Fixed by p̂ alone: it tells you nothing the accuracy did not already tell you, and it does not shrink as N grows. An SD bar on an accuracy chart is almost always a mistake. (An SD across repeated runs or folds is a different quantity and can be legitimate.)

Standard error

How much the observed accuracy would scatter across fresh test sets of size N.

SE = SD / √N

—

A statement about the estimate, not about individual answers. It shrinks with √N: 100× the data buys 10× the precision. The last section shows it as the spread of a histogram.

95% confidence interval

The interval the recipe produces from this one eval.

Wilson score interval

—

Not: “95% probability the true accuracy is in here”. But: “repeat the eval; 95% of the intervals built this way catch the true accuracy.” A property of the recipe, not of this interval. For a probability about the accuracy you need a belief: see Estimating Accuracy.

Notes

Population SD vs. sample SD

√(p̂(1−p̂)) is the standard deviation of a correct/wrong outcome with mean p̂. A spreadsheet's STDEV on the N zeros and ones uses an n − 1 denominator and gives √(N/(N−1)) × that: 0.447 instead of 0.400 for 4 out of 5, and 0.402 for 80 out of 100. The card above shows both.

Wald vs. Wilson

The textbook interval p̂ ± 1.96·SE (Wald) assumes the observed accuracy is normally distributed around the truth, which is fine at N = 100 and breaks at small N or extreme accuracy: for 4 out of 5 it runs from 45% to 115%, and its coverage falls well below 95%. The Wilson interval solves for the accuracies that would make the observed p̂ plausible, stays inside 0 to 100%, and keeps its coverage honest.

Three misreadings of a 95% CI
  • “There is a 95% probability the true accuracy is in this interval.” The truth is a fixed number; it is either in there or it is not. The 95% describes how often the recipe succeeds.
  • “95% of runs will land in this range.” That treats the CI like an SD-style spread of outcomes. It is not.
  • “Two overlapping CIs mean no significant difference.” Overlap is a much more conservative test than it looks.
