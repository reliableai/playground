Climbing on Noisy Labels

The same wrong labels do three different things depending on where they sit. In the training set the climb learns them. In the validation set they mislead your checks. In the test set they move the number you report.

How to read and interact with this page
  1. Each section puts the wrong labels in one set, and the other sets stay clean, so you see what each set’s labels do on their own. The fourth section puts one labeler’s habit in all three.
  2. Blue, purple and grey are scores you can see; orange is the truth: how often the router is right on new tickets, which you normally cannot see.
  3. “Run one climb” draws a new world each time. The buttons that run 200 or 500 climbs use fixed seeds, so they always give the same numbers.
  4. Try first: in the first section, run 200 climbs at every rate with the reviewer off, then read the table.

The router and its labels

We route support tickets to one of three teams: Billing, Access or Network. The data comes in three sets: 200 training tickets, 100 validation tickets and 100 test tickets. Each ticket’s label is the team that solved it, and sometimes that was the wrong team.

Tickets come in 30 patterns, a few common and many rare. The first prompt routes each pattern right 60% to 95% of the time, about 78% overall. Then an optimizer climbs on the training set. Each round it runs the prompt, reads every miss (a ticket where the answer differs from the label) and writes a rule for it.

A rule always fixes the ticket it was written for. With probability g, the fix generality, it is also a rule for the whole pattern, and it moves the pattern halfway toward the team in the label. If the label was right, the pattern improves on new tickets. If the label was wrong, the rule sends the pattern to the wrong team.

A wrong label comes in two kinds. Random slips are spread over tickets, and each goes to one of the other two teams. A labeler’s habit gets a few patterns wrong every time, always the same way.

2 · Wrong labels in validation: the check misreads

Now training and test are clean, and some validation labels are wrong. Nobody reads validation tickets and no rule is written from them, so the climb itself is unaffected. What changes is what validation tells you about the climb.

A gap that is not overfitting

We score validation after every round to check the climb. A training score that pulls away from validation is the usual sign of overfitting. Wrong validation labels pull the two apart on their own.

Picking a strategy

Before the climb we fix four strategies: four optimizers whose rules generalize 10%, 30%, 50% and 70% of the time. Each one climbs on the clean training set. Validation scores the four final prompts once, and we keep the one that scores highest. Wrong labels shrink every difference validation sees, while chance moves a 100-ticket score as much as before.

The cost of a pick is how many points of true accuracy it loses against the truly best of the four. A real difference between two prompts shows on noisy labels as that difference times 1 − 1.5 × (share of wrong labels), with three teams and slips spread evenly.

4 · One labeler for all three sets: a habit passes every check

Random slips in validation and test drag the score down. A habit does something worse when the same labeler labeled all three sets. The climb learns the habit on training, and validation and test reward it, because their labels share it.
